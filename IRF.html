01 · Reading an impulse response

What is an Impulse Response Function?

An Impulse Response Function traces the dynamic response of a variable to an unexpected shock.

A shock happens today. What happens to the variable on impact — and what remains 1, 2, 3, ... periods later?

How large is the shock?

A common convention is to report the response to a positive one-standard-deviation shock. This is a normalization, not a requirement. Depending on the economic question, we can use a negative shock or a one-unit shock.

How do we read it? Look at the impact response, its sign, its maximum effect, how persistent it is, whether it changes sign, when it approaches zero, and whether the confidence interval includes zero.
02 · Formalization

Forecast with the shock − forecast without the shock

An IRF is a counterfactual comparison between two forecasts that are identical except for the innovation introduced today.

\[\boxed{IRF(h;\delta)=E_t(y_{t+h}\mid u_t=\delta)-E_t(y_{t+h}\mid u_t=0)}\]

Propagate the effect recursively

For an AR(1),

\[y_t=c+\phi y_{t-1}+u_t\]

after a unit shock today and no additional shocks, the effect is propagated recursively:

Impact
\(IRF(0)=1\)
h = 1
\(IRF(1)=\phi\)
h = 2
\(IRF(2)=\phi^2\)
h = 3
\(IRF(3)=\phi^3\)
h
\(IRF(h)=\phi^h\)
Connection with mean reversion. The same \(\phi^h\) that described the decay of a deviation from the long-run mean now describes the propagation of a shock.

Changing sign or magnitude

Econometric software commonly reports the response to a positive one-standard-deviation shock, \(IRF_{+1\sigma}(h)\). In a linear model, other shock normalizations can be obtained directly from that output.

Standard output
\[IRF_{+1\sigma}(h)\]
Negative shock
\[IRF_{-1\sigma}(h)=-IRF_{+1\sigma}(h)\]
Multiply the entire IRF by \(-1\).
One-unit shock
\[IRF_{+1}(h)=\frac{IRF_{+1\sigma}(h)}{\hat{\sigma}_u}\]
Divide the entire IRF by the estimated standard deviation of the innovation.
\[\boxed{IRF_k(h)=\frac{k}{\hat{\sigma}_u}IRF_{+1\sigma}(h)}\]
03 · Statistical uncertainty

The IRF is estimated

We do not know the true model parameters. We estimate them from a sample, so the estimated impulse response is uncertain too.

\[\widehat{IRF}(h)=\hat\phi^h\]
How much could the impulse response change because the estimated model parameters are uncertain?

Monte Carlo intuition

After estimating the model, we have estimated parameters \(\hat\theta\) and their estimated covariance matrix. We repeatedly draw plausible parameter values from their estimated distribution and recompute the IRF.

\[\theta^{(b)}\sim N\!\left(\hat\theta,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\theta)\right)\]

\[IRF^{(1)}(h),\;IRF^{(2)}(h),\;\ldots,\;IRF^{(B)}(h)\]

At each horizon, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated IRFs form a 95% Monte Carlo confidence interval.

Monte Carlo intuition. The coefficients are estimated with uncertainty. By repeatedly drawing plausible coefficient values and recomputing the IRF, we see how parameter uncertainty translates into uncertainty about the dynamic response. Econometric software performs the IRF calculations for us.

06 · Looking ahead

What changes in a multivariate model?

In a univariate model there is only one innovation, so we know which shock generated the response.

What happens when several variables — and several shocks — interact simultaneously?
That is the next challenge. In VAR models, the idea of an impulse response stays the same. The new problem is identifying which economic shock we are actually studying.
