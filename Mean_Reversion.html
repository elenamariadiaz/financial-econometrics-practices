02 · Mean reversion

A simple dynamic idea

A mean-reverting process is not constant. It can move substantially through time, but deviations from its long-run level tend to disappear.

\[ y_t = c+\phi y_{t-1}+u_t \]

Where is the long-run mean?

If the process is stationary, its unconditional mean is constant. Let \[ E(y_t)=E(y_{t-1})=\mu. \]

\[ \mu=c+\phi\mu \]
↓
\[ \mu(1-\phi)=c \]
↓
\[ \boxed{ \mu=\frac{c}{1-\phi} } \]

Now rewrite the process around its mean

\[ c=\mu(1-\phi) \]
↓
\[ y_t = \mu(1-\phi) + \phi y_{t-1} + u_t \]
↓
\[ y_t = \mu + \phi(y_{t-1}-\mu) + u_t \]
↓
\[ \boxed{ y_t-\mu = \phi(y_{t-1}-\mu) + u_t } \]
Key idea. The deviation from the long-run mean today depends on the deviation yesterday. When \( |\phi|<1 \), those deviations tend to shrink over time. The closer \( |\phi| \) is to one, the more persistent the process.

Why an AR(1)?

After studying ARMA models, we know that an AR(1) is not necessarily the best forecasting model for every interest-rate series. Here we use the AR(1) as a simple benchmark: its coefficient \(\phi\) gives us a transparent measure of persistence and allows us to quantify the speed of mean reversion.

03 · Persistence

How quickly does a deviation from the mean disappear?

Suppose the process begins one unit away from its long-run mean and no new shocks arrive. The subsequent deviations are

\[ 1,\quad \phi,\quad \phi^2,\quad \phi^3,\quad \dots \]
\(\phi\) 0.80 Half-life: 3.11 periods

Half-life

The half-life tells us how many periods are required for a deviation from the long-run mean to be reduced by half. Starting from a deviation equal to one, after \(h\) periods the remaining deviation is \(\phi^h\).

\[ \phi^h=0.5 \]
↓
\[ h\ln(|\phi|)=\ln(0.5) \]
↓
\[ \boxed{ HL=\frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(|\phi|)} } \]
Stationarity and speed of mean reversion are not the same thing. Two stationary processes can both satisfy \( |\phi|<1 \) and yet have very different levels of persistence.

04 · From one rate to the term structure

But interest rates do not fluctuate alone.

The 10-year rate is only one point on a much richer object: the yield curve.

Interest rates across maturities

Treasury yields with different maturities evolve through time. They are not identical, but their movements are strongly connected.

U.S. Treasury yields from 1 month to 30 years. Monthly observations. Source: FRED.
If these interest rates move together, perhaps we should think of them not as eleven independent time series, but as one evolving object.

The yield curve through time

At every point in time, yields across maturities form a curve. Stack those curves through time, and we obtain a yield surface.

What happens if we freeze time and look at the curve on a particular date?

Different moments, different curves

The shape of the yield curve changes through time. Sometimes it is steep. Sometimes it is flat. Sometimes it is inverted. Sometimes its middle changes relative to both ends.

Selected historical Treasury yield curves.
Look beyond individual rates. When comparing yield curves across dates, three types of movements repeatedly appear: the entire curve can move, its steepness can change, and its middle can bend.

Three intuitive movements

Level

Up or down

Most maturities move in approximately the same direction. The entire yield curve shifts upward or downward.

Slope

Steeper or flatter

Short- and long-term rates move differently. The steepness of the yield curve changes.

Curvature

The middle bends

Intermediate maturities move differently from the short and long ends of the curve.

Can we describe these movements with only a few numbers?
05 · Describing the curve

Level, slope and curvature

Nelson–Siegel provides a parsimonious way to summarize an entire yield curve using three factors.

\[ \boxed{ y_t(\tau) = \beta_{1t} + \beta_{2t} \left( \frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}} {\lambda\tau} \right) + \beta_{3t} \left[ \frac{1-e^{-\lambda\tau}} {\lambda\tau} - e^{-\lambda\tau} \right] } \]

where \(\tau\) denotes maturity.

How are the factors estimated?

We fix \(\lambda\), so the factor loadings are known at each maturity. The model is therefore linear in \(\beta_{1t}\), \(\beta_{2t}\), and \(\beta_{3t}\). At each date, we estimate the three factors by cross-sectional OLS:

\[ \mathbf{y}_t = X\boldsymbol{\beta}_t + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_t = (X'X)^{-1}X'\mathbf{y}_t. \]
11 observed Treasury yields → 3 estimated factors. At each month, the yields observed across maturities are summarized by Level, Slope and Curvature. Repeating the same cross-sectional regression every month produces three time series of estimated factors.

We fix \(\lambda=0.7308\), equivalent to \(0.0609\) when maturity is measured in months, following Diebold and Li. This places the maximum curvature loading at approximately 30 months.

\(\beta_{1t}\)

Level

Its loading is equal to one at every maturity. It therefore shifts the whole curve.

\(\beta_{2t}\)

Slope

Its loading is largest at short maturities and gradually approaches zero.

Sign convention: \(\beta_{2t}\approx y_t(\mathrm{short})-y_t(\mathrm{long})\). Therefore, a negative slope factor typically corresponds to an upward-sloping yield curve.

\(\beta_{3t}\)

Curvature

Its loading is small at the short and long ends and largest at intermediate maturities.

Why do the factors have these interpretations?

Nelson–Siegel factor loadings across maturity.
The interpretation comes from the loadings. Level affects every maturity similarly. Slope primarily changes the short end relative to the long end. Curvature primarily affects intermediate maturities.
